Her legacy · Women who shaped nations

We come from women who shaped nations.

Thirteen Black women across the Americas and Africa, and the writers who kept the record, who freed people, built movements, defended what was sacred and changed the course of their countries. What each did, what it cost, and what to carry forward.

The Americas
Maryland, USA1822–1913Liberator

Harriet Tubman

Born enslaved, she freed herself, then kept going back for others.

What she did

  • Escaped slavery alone in 1849 and reached Philadelphia.
  • Returned to Maryland’s Eastern Shore 13 times between 1850 and 1860 and freed more than 70 family members and friends.
  • On June 1, 1863, led the Combahee River Raid in South Carolina, freeing more than 750 people: the first woman to lead such a raid in U.S. military history.
  • Co-founded the National Association of Colored Women, which fought for Black women’s equality and the vote.

What it cost her

Every trip back risked recapture or death.

Carry it forward

Freedom isn’t finished when you have yours. Go back for your people.

Source: National Park Service, Harriet Tubman Underground Railroad National Historical Park

Mississippi, Tennessee & Illinois, USA1862–1931Journalist

Ida B. Wells

She documented what others refused to name, and the record she kept still stands.

What she did

  • Born enslaved in Holly Springs, Mississippi.
  • In 1884, refused to give up her first-class train seat; she was removed by force and sued.
  • After three friends were lynched in Memphis in 1892, launched an anti-lynching campaign built on reporting and evidence.
  • Considered a founder of the NAACP; in 1913 founded the Alpha Suffrage Club in Chicago so Black women could elect leaders who would serve their community.
  • Awarded a Pulitzer Prize special citation in 2020 for her reporting.

What it cost her

A mob destroyed her newspaper office in Memphis while she was away.

Carry it forward

Facts are a form of protection. Write it down, publish it, keep the record.

Source: National Park Service, Ida B. Wells

Mississippi, USA1917–1977Organizer

Fannie Lou Hamer

A sharecropper who tried to register to vote, and changed what a nation had to look at.

What she did

  • The youngest of 20 children of sharecroppers; worked a Sunflower County plantation for about 18 years.
  • Tried to register to vote in 1962 with SNCC organizers.
  • Helped organize the Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party in 1964.
  • Testified before the Democratic National Convention that year about the violence she faced for trying to vote: “If the Freedom Democratic Party is not seated now, I question America.”

What it cost her

Fired and forced from her home for registering; beaten in a Winona jail in 1963, leaving kidney damage and partial blindness.

Carry it forward

You don’t need a title to change history. You need the courage to stand up and tell the truth in public.

Source: Martin Luther King, Jr. Research and Education Institute, Stanford University

Virginia & North Carolina, USA1903–1986Strategist

Ella Baker

The organizer behind the organizers, who believed people could lead themselves.

What she did

  • Directed NAACP branches in the 1940s, building a network that later powered voter registration in Mississippi.
  • Served as executive secretary of the Southern Christian Leadership Conference.
  • Organized the 1960 founding conference at Shaw University that became SNCC, and urged the students to form their own organization rather than a wing of an existing one.
  • Taught bottom-up, group-centered leadership: “Strong people don’t need strong leaders.”

How she led

Mostly behind the scenes, building the people and structures others would lead from.

Carry it forward

Build leaders, not followers. The work outlasts any one person when everyone can carry it.

Source: SNCC Digital Gateway, Ella Baker

Brooklyn, New York, USA1924–2005Trailblazer

Shirley Chisholm

The daughter of Barbadian immigrants who walked into Congress, then ran for president.

What she did

  • In 1968, became the first Black woman elected to the U.S. Congress.
  • Represented New York’s 12th district for seven terms, 1969 to 1983.
  • A founding member of the Congressional Black Caucus in 1971.
  • In 1972, became the first Black candidate to seek a major party’s presidential nomination, and the first woman to seek the Democratic nomination, under the slogan “Unbought and Unbossed.”
  • Awarded the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 2015.

How she led

Mostly behind the scenes, building the people and structures others would lead from.

Carry it forward

If they don’t give you a seat at the table, bring a folding chair. Then run for the head of it.

Source: U.S. National Archives, Shirley Chisholm

Blue Mountains, Jamaicadied 1733Freedom fighter

Queen Nanny

Taken from West Africa, she built a free town in the mountains and defended it.

What she did

  • Widely accepted to have come from the Ashanti people of present-day Ghana.
  • With her brother Quao, founded Nanny Town in Jamaica’s Blue Mountains.
  • Farmed and traded, and led raids on plantations that helped about 1,000 people escape slavery.
  • A spiritual leader and Obeah practitioner as well as a military one.
  • Named a National Hero of Jamaica, the only woman among the seven, and pictured on the $500 note.

What it cost her

Killed in 1733 during the war with the British; Nanny Town was destroyed the next year.

Carry it forward

Spiritual strength and practical strategy belong together. Build the refuge, then defend it.

Source: BlackPast.org, Queen Nanny of the Maroons

Africa
Ndongo & Matamba (Angola)c. 1583–1663Queen & strategist

Queen Njinga

A diplomat and warrior queen who held off an empire for decades.

What she did

  • In 1622, negotiated with the Portuguese governor in Luanda; the story is told that she had a servant kneel so she could sit level with him.
  • Ruled Ndongo from 1624, then conquered Matamba and joined the kingdoms.
  • Gave refuge to people who escaped slavery, built armies and fought the Portuguese for decades, allying with the Dutch and Kongo.
  • Won a treaty around 1656 in which Portugal recognized her as ruler of Matamba and Ndongo.

What it cost her

Her record is complex: to secure her state she also traded in enslaved people and paid tribute in captives. Portuguese and Angolan accounts remember her very differently.

Carry it forward

Study power closely, including where our heroes compromised. An honest record makes us stronger, not weaker.

Source: South African History Online, Njinga Ana de Sousa

Ejisu, Ashanti (Ghana)c. 1840–1921Queen mother & war leader

Yaa Asantewaa

When the British demanded the Golden Stool, she called her nation to defend it.

What she did

  • Queen Mother of Ejisu, a role tied to protecting the Golden Stool, the sacred symbol of Ashanti nationhood.
  • In 1900, rallied Ashanti forces against British rule in the War of the Golden Stool, the only woman to hold the Ashanti war-leader role.
  • The rebellion besieged the British fort at Kumasi for months.
  • Her name lives on in a girls’ school in Kumasi, a London cultural centre, and a portrait installed at the British Museum in 2024.

What it cost her

Captured and exiled to the Seychelles, where she died in 1921; her remains were later returned to Ghana for a royal burial.

Carry it forward

Some things are sacred. Know what yours are, and be ready to stand for them.

Source: Yaa Asantewaa, encyclopedic entry (Wikipedia)

Abeokuta (Nigeria)1900–1978Organizer

Funmilayo Ransome-Kuti

She organized market women into a force that toppled a king’s tax.

What she did

  • Led the Abeokuta Women’s Union, which used petitions, vigils, refusals to pay and mass demonstrations against unfair taxes on market women.
  • Those campaigns led the Alake of Egbaland to step down temporarily in 1949.
  • Took part in Nigeria’s independence movement, including a 1947 delegation to London, and helped found national women’s federations.
  • Awarded the Lenin Peace Prize in 1970.

What it cost her

In 1977, soldiers raided her family’s compound and threw her from a second-floor window; she died of her injuries in 1978.

Carry it forward

Organized women are a political power. Start with the women in your own market, church or block.

Source: Funmilayo Ransome-Kuti, encyclopedic entry (Wikipedia)

Nyeri (Kenya)1940–2011Scientist & activist

Wangari Maathai

She started with trees and ended up changing a nation’s politics.

What she did

  • The first woman in East and Central Africa to earn a doctorate.
  • Began the tree-planting work in 1976 that grew into the Green Belt Movement, with women’s groups planting more than 20 million trees.
  • Elected to Kenya’s parliament in 2002 with 98% of the vote and became Assistant Minister for Environment.
  • Awarded the 2004 Nobel Peace Prize.

How she led

Mostly behind the scenes, building the people and structures others would lead from.

Carry it forward

Care for the land and the community, and the community will carry the change.

Source: The Nobel Prize, Wangari Maathai biographical

Voices · the writers who kept the record
Florida, Harlem, Haiti & Jamaica1891–1960Writer & anthropologist

Zora Neale Hurston

She recorded our folklore and our spiritual traditions in our own voice.

What she did

  • Earned her anthropology degree from Barnard College in 1928, after research with Franz Boas.
  • Collected African American folklore across the South in Mules and Men.
  • With a Guggenheim Fellowship, studied spiritual practice in Jamaica and Haiti, published as Tell My Horse.
  • Wrote Their Eyes Were Watching God, one of the most loved novels of Black womanhood.

What it cost her

Critics gave her Caribbean work a lukewarm reception; she died in 1960 and was buried in an unmarked grave in Fort Pierce, Florida.

Carry it forward

Our spiritual traditions are knowledge, not superstition. Record them in our own words before others record them for us.

Source: JSTOR Daily, Voodoo and the Work of Zora Neale Hurston

Eatonton, Georgia, USAborn 1944Writer & womanist

Alice Walker

A sharecropper’s daughter who registered voters, won the Pulitzer, and brought Zora back.

What she did

  • Registered Black voters in Georgia and joined voter registration campaigns across the Deep South as a student.
  • Wrote The Color Purple (1982), which won the Pulitzer Prize and the National Book Award in 1983.
  • Introduced the word womanist in In Search of Our Mothers’ Gardens (1983), for Black feminists who prize women’s inventiveness and resilience.
  • Went looking for Zora Neale Hurston’s grave in 1973, and her writing and 1979 Hurston anthology brought Hurston back into print.

How she led

Mostly behind the scenes, building the people and structures others would lead from.

Carry it forward

Go looking for the women who came before you. Honor them, and put their names back in print.

Source: New Georgia Encyclopedia, Alice Walker

Knoxville, Tennessee & Virginia, USA1943–2024Poet

Nikki Giovanni

A poet of the Black Arts Movement who taught generations to love themselves out loud.

What she did

  • Her first books, Black Feeling, Black Talk (1968) and Black Judgment (1969), carried the fire of the Black Arts Movement.
  • Taught at Virginia Tech from 1987, named University Distinguished Professor in 1999, and remained a professor there until her death.
  • Won seven NAACP Image Awards for Literature and the first Rosa Parks Woman of Courage Award.

How she led

Mostly behind the scenes, building the people and structures others would lead from.

Carry it forward

Say who you are, in your own words, with your whole chest. Then teach someone else to do it.

Source: Academy of American Poets, Nikki Giovanni
