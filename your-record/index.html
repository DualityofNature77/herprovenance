Why records win

In disputes over credit, pay or treatment, the person with dated, specific records usually has the stronger hand.

In Global Health Solutions v. Selner (2025), a federal appeals court upheld a patent ruling that turned on timestamps: the inventor’s dated emails showed he had the idea at 12:55 PM, before the other side’s 4:04 PM email, and he kept his patent.Source: Federal Circuit, Global Health Solutions LLC v. Selner (Aug. 26, 2025), summarized by White & Case

Patents now go to whoever files first, so the winning move is both: record your work the moment you make it, and file. If someone takes your idea and files ahead of you, your dated record is the evidence they got it from you.

How to document, step by step

  1. Start today. Details fade and deadlines run. Write down what you remember now, with dates as close as you can get them.
  2. Be specific. Date, time, place, who was there, the exact words, how you responded, and what changed afterward. “My boss was rude again” won’t help you; the exact sentence will.
  3. Write it close to the event. Notes made the same day carry more weight than a story reconstructed months later.
  4. Keep it off work systems. Use a personal device or a notebook, not a work laptop, email or chat your employer can read or delete.
  5. Put verbal things in writing. After a conversation or a complaint, send a short email: “To confirm what we discussed today…”
  6. Keep your performance history. Reviews, raises and praise from before the problem started, and how similar colleagues were treated.
  7. Don’t take what isn’t yours. Record what happened to you; don’t copy confidential company or client data.
  8. Build a timeline. One chronological list that points to each supporting note, email and file.
These steps follow the guidance employment lawyers give clients: contemporaneous, specific notes kept off work devices, written follow-up to verbal complaints, and performance and comparison records alongside incident notes.Source: Schwartz & Perry LLP, How to document workplace discrimination effectively (Jan. 2026)

Your rights and deadlines (United States)

Federal law prohibits job discrimination based on sex, race, color, religion, national origin, age (40 and over), disability and genetic information, and it prohibits retaliation for complaining about it.

  • Deadline: you generally have 180 calendar days to file a charge with the EEOC. It extends to 300 days if a state or local agency enforces a law against the same kind of discrimination. (For age claims, only a state law and agency extend it.)
  • How to file: online through the EEOC Public Portal, in person at one of 53 EEOC offices, or by mail with a signed letter. You can start by phone at 1-800-669-4000, but charges aren’t taken over the phone. Your state’s fair employment agency can file it too.
  • You don’t need a lawyer to file, though talking to an employment lawyer early, with your timeline in hand, often helps.
  • Federal employees follow a different process with shorter deadlines.
Deadlines, filing routes and phone number from the EEOC.Source: U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission, How to File a Charge of Employment Discrimination

Outside the U.S.? Most countries have an equality body or labor ministry that takes complaints, often with short deadlines. Search for your country’s equal-treatment or human-rights commission and start your record today either way.
