Winning women

Know what’s best about you.

Our foremothers were seen as strong, capable, faithful and organized, because they were. Winning women carry that forward: not winning against each other, but winning together.

Know your worth

Excellence isn’t something we have to prove we deserve. It’s inherited.

  • Name your gifts out loud. Write down what you’re known for, what you’ve built and what it was worth. Read it before every review and every negotiation.
  • Price your work. Black women working full time are paid 64 cents for every dollar paid to white men. Ask for the pay band in writing, and ask for the top of it.
  • Refuse the “strong Black woman” trap. Strength is ours. Being expected to carry everything for everyone, unpaid and uncredited, is not.

How we treat each other

The movement isn’t only out there. It’s in how we speak to and about each other.

  • Credit each other by name, in the meeting, in the group chat, in the room you’re in and she isn’t.
  • Share the room. When you get in, hold the door: introductions, referrals, the stretch assignment.
  • Refuse the tear-down. No public dragging of a sister for clout. Disagree in private, defend in public.
  • Keep our word. Show up when you say you will. Reliability is respect, and it’s how trust gets built across a community.
  • Protect our girls. Teach them the history in Her Legacy before anyone else tells them who they are.

Spirit as strategy

Queen Nanny was a spiritual leader and a military one. Yaa Asantewaa went to war for a sacred stool. Fannie Lou Hamer sang hymns in the face of violence.

For the spiritually aware woman, faith and activism were never separate. Whatever your practice, whether church, ancestral tradition, meditation or Maat, it is part of how you sustain the work. Kemet joined spirit, law and daily life into one system; read how.

  • Rest is resistance. Burnout serves no one. Schedule rest like a meeting.
  • Remember your ancestors. Learn their names. Tell their stories. Let them steady you.
  • Align your actions with your values. TMBOL’s daily practice of the 42 Laws of Maat is one path. Explore it at TMBOL.org.

Keep the receipts

Ida B. Wells won with evidence. Do the same: keep a dated record of your ideas, your work and how you were treated. Here’s exactly how, and how The Record will make it simple.

Pass it on

Ella Baker said strong people don’t need strong leaders. Share what you learn here with one woman this week: a niece, a coworker, a sister in your congregation. That’s how a legacy keeps moving.
