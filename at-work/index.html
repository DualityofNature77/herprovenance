At work

Name the pattern. Keep the record.

For Black women, sexism and racism at work arrive together. Here are the six patterns, how to spot them, and what to do about each.

Compared with white, non-Hispanic men working full time, year-round in 2025, Black women earned 64 cents on the dollar, and the gap widened that year. Only 46 percent of companies say advancing women of color is a high priority.Sources: Center for American Progress, Sept. 15, 2026, analysis of U.S. Census Bureau 2025 income data; LeanIn.Org & McKinsey, Women in the Workplace 2025 (70 companies, 9,500+ employees), as reported by HR Dive
Pattern 01

Credit theft

Your idea is presented in the meeting by someone else. Your work ships under another name.

What it looks like

  • Your proposal resurfaces a week later as a colleague’s idea.
  • Your name is missing from the deck, the paper, the patent or the launch email.
  • “We” did it when it went well; “you” did it when it didn’t.

What to do

  • Record each idea, draft and file the day you make it, with a date no one can dispute.
  • Send a short recap after meetings: “Following up on the plan I proposed Tuesday…”
  • Name your work out loud: “Building on the model I shared…”
  • Keep a running wins file for reviews and promotion cases.
In a study of 128,859 people on 9,778 research teams, women were far less likely than men to be named as authors, and the gap on patents was 59 percent, even after controlling for other factors.Source: Ross et al., “Women are credited less in science than men,” Nature, 2022 (128,859 people on 9,778 research teams)
Pattern 02

Authority erosion

Your expertise is questioned in rooms where the same words from a man would not be.

What it looks like

  • You’re interrupted, talked over, or asked to “double-check” with someone junior.
  • Your decision is reversed without a conversation.
  • Clients or vendors are steered to a male colleague.

What to do

  • Put decisions and decision rights in writing, before and after the meeting.
  • Agree with allies to amplify: repeat each other’s points and credit them by name.
  • When interrupted, finish the sentence: “I’ll finish the point, then I want your view.”
  • Log reversals with dates and who decided.
Pattern 03

Pay architecture

The pay gap isn’t an accident. It’s built into titles, negotiations and promotion criteria, and it compounds.

What it looks like

  • Same work, a lower title, a lower band.
  • Raises tied to “negotiation skill” that is punished when women use it.
  • Starting-pay gaps that grow with every percentage raise.

What to do

  • Ask in writing for the pay band for your role and level.
  • Keep every offer letter, title change and raise with dates.
  • Compare yourself with colleagues doing similar work (your comparators) and note differences.
  • Check whether your state requires employers to post or share pay ranges.
In 2025, women working full time, year-round earned 84 cents for every dollar men earned. Compared with white, non-Hispanic men, Black women earned 64 cents and Hispanic women 60 cents.Source: Center for American Progress, Sept. 15, 2026, analysis of U.S. Census Bureau 2025 income data
Pattern 04

Documentation gaps

When you need to prove what happened, the emails are gone and the agreement was “verbal.”

What it looks like

  • Promises made in hallways and never written down.
  • Meetings with no notes, or notes kept by the other side.
  • Records on systems your employer controls and can delete.

What to do

  • After any verbal agreement, send a one-paragraph email confirming it.
  • Keep your own dated log on a personal device, never a work account.
  • Write specifics: date, who was there, exact words, what changed after.
  • Don’t copy confidential company data; record what happened to you.
Employment lawyers stress the same points: contemporaneous, specific, dated notes kept off work systems, plus written follow-up to verbal complaints.Source: Schwartz & Perry LLP, How to document workplace discrimination effectively (Jan. 2026)
Pattern 05

Isolation

The decisions get made at the dinners, off-sites and hallway conversations you weren’t invited to.

What it looks like

  • The “informal” planning meeting happens without you.
  • You hear about a reorg or an opening after it’s decided.
  • No one senior will put their name behind yours.

What to do

  • Ask directly to be included, and note when you aren’t.
  • Build a network outside your team and company.
  • Look for a sponsor: someone senior who will say your name in rooms you’re not in.
  • Share information with other women and newcomers; isolation only works if we let it.
The latest Women in the Workplace report found that when women and men get sponsorship and similar manager support, they are equally enthusiastic about promotion.Source: LeanIn.Org & McKinsey, Women in the Workplace 2025 (70 companies, 9,500+ employees), as reported by HR Dive
Pattern 06

The penalty box

Speak up and you’re difficult. Document and you’re paranoid. Succeed and you’re lucky.

What it looks like

  • Feedback about your “tone” instead of your work.
  • A good review turns bad right after you raise a concern.
  • Your success is credited to timing, luck or a mentor.

What to do

  • Ask for specific examples whenever feedback is about style, not results.
  • Keep reviews from before and after any complaint you make.
  • Know that retaliating against someone for raising discrimination is itself illegal under federal law.
  • Write down the outcome of your work, with numbers, as it happens.
When a pattern becomes a case: if it’s tied to your race, sex or another protected characteristic, or it follows a complaint you made, it may be illegal discrimination or retaliation, and you can name both race and sex. Federal charges have deadlines as short as 180 days. See your rights and deadlines.
